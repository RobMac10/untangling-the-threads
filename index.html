Meet the Editor

In 2025 I am untangling our ancestral tapestry. As satellites map the world and digital archives and data bases bridge continents, I try to unravel the threads that make up The Story of US. These tales connect us to the people and places of the past as well as to each other in the most surprising ways.

Meet the Editor:

On January 25, 1975, Robin Truasheim and Peter McKean were joined in marriage at St Andrew’s Anglican Church in South Brisbane, a union that marked the beginning of their shared journey. In 2025, they uncovered a remarkable connection: a shared lineage tracing back to a mutual 3rd great-grandfather, William Mortimer (born 1805), and his wife, their 3rd great-grandmother, Fanny Kellett (born 1810). Through Frances, and William, we can explore The Story of Us, a rich tapestry that weaves in our English gentry ancestors—the illustrious Cromwell and Ingoldsby families—revealing a heritage steeped in history and legacy.

Editor’s Note: The Kellett-Mortimer Journey from Cavan to Queensland

This is an amazing tale of Fanny (Frances) Kellett, born in 1810 in Cornashesk, Virginia, County Cavan, to Mary Bell and Charles Kellett, linen traders rooted in Lurgan’s bustling heart. Across the county in Mullagh, was William Mortimer, born 1805 to gentleman farmer David, growing up amidst flax fields and Protestant gentry. Their paths entwined, and on December 22, 1837, they wed in Dublin’s St. Michan’s, forging a union that would span continents. In Mullagh, they raised five children until the Great Famine (1845–1852) ravaged Ireland, rotting potatoes and dimming the linen trade. With ancestral Ingoldsby grit tracing back to our Cromwell kin, they set their sights on Queensland’s promise of land and opportunity, and both dreamed of charting a new course to Australia’s shores.

Their daughter Mary Frances, born December 29, 1841, in Lislin, Mullagh, married Robert Dawson Soden of Virginia, Cavan, in Balbriggan in 1862. At twenty, pregnant and bold, she joined her parents, William and Fanny, on the Cairngorm, a Black Ball ship captained by Hugh Auld, under Queensland’s assisted emigration scheme. Departing February 1863, the 92-day voyage was gruelling—steerage reeked, storms raged, and equatorial heat stifled. Mary Frances gave birth to Catherine (Kate) Soden on April 6, 1863, in the Indian Ocean, with a midwife’s aid and blankets for a cradle. Kate was one of three births aboard the voyage, which included the arrival of two girls and a boy. On July 2, 1863, the Cairngorm docked at Moreton Bay. Mary and Robert, with three-month-old Kate, settled at Pine Mountain near Ipswich, later farming corn and lucerne on 160 acres at Mt Beppo. They raised ten children: Kate, their eldest, grew up, married William Boneham in 1887, giving birth to twelve—William, James, John, Robert, Charles, Richard, Alice, George, Edward, Elizabeth, Kate and Arthur—before passing in Somerset in 1931.

Daughter Letitia, born 1844, also sailed to Brisbane, marrying Alexander Martin on September 27, 1866, at St. John’s, now the Anglican cathedral in Brisbane. Alexander, born in 1840 in Rathfriland, County Down, had arrived on the ‘Duke of Newcastle’ in 1862, enduring steerage for his farmer’s dream. In Barolin near Bundaberg, they worked sugar-rich red soil, raising nine children over seventeen years. Their labour helped transform Bundaberg into Queensland’s sugar hub by the 1870s.

From Cavan’s green hills to Australia’s frontier, the Kellett-Mortimer legacy we share endures, a testament to resilience and hope, and makes up the Story of Us.

The Story of 3rd Great Grandmother Fanny (Frances) Kellett.

I was born in 1810 to Charles and Mary Bell Kellett, and in ‘37 wed William Mortimer, God love him, in Dublin’s St. Michan’s. In Mullagh, County Cavan, we made our home, a wee town cradled by hills. The 1840s near broke us, with family tragedy, then the hunger that swept Ireland like a banshee’s wail. A young mother I was, my heart stitched to our babes with my joys in a world gone dark.

Mullagh was a small bit of a place, a market town with stone houses huddled tight, a fairground for cattle, and fields that rolled to the sky. Our house sat near Mullagh Lough, where the heather’s scent was sweet as a summer’s dream, afore the blight turned all to ash. Your great granda William, a landed Mortimer to be sure, born in 1805, was a steady soul, his hands calloused from the land and the loom, weaving linen as my kin did. Our Protestant faith, rooted deep in our Cromwellian. Ingoldsby and Mortimer blood, was my anchor.

Though we were but a handful among the local Catholic folk, we too knelt at St. Kilian’s Church, its plain walls a shelter… not yet the fine place it’d grow to be.

Before the ’40s, oh, life had a shine to it! Our first wee one we named Charles, came in ’39, and the others, Mary Frances ‘41, Fanny ‘43, Letitia ’44 (the saddest of years) and James ‘51 followed. I’ spent my time crooning by the fire, spinning yarns of the Ó Raghallaighs, O’Rourkes and Cavan’s old lords and Princes of East Breifne, their castles nearby in ruins.

Editor’s Note:

The Ó Raghallaighs (O’Reillys) were a prominent Gaelic Irish family in the region of Breifne, which includes Mullagh in County Cavan, during its early history.

Mullagh, means "the hilltop" in Irish (An Mullach), is a town in County Cavan with a history stretching back centuries. The Ó Raghallaighs emerged as a significant family in the region during the medieval period. Originating as a sept of the Muintir Maelmórdha tribe in Breifne, they initially served as vassals to the more dominant O’Rourkes. By the early 12th century, the Ó Raghallaighs were tasked with controlling Machaire Gaileng (the plain of the Gailenga, an area that includes parts of modern-day Mullagh) on behalf of the O’Rourkes. This arrangement positioned them as a subordinate but influential family in the region.

The Ó Raghallaighs’ fortunes changed dramatically when Tiernán O’Rourke, King of Breifne, was betrayed and killed by Hugh de Lacy in Athboy in 1172. Seizing the opportunity, the Ó Raghallaighs allied with de Lacy, driving the O’Rourkes into western Breifne and briefly taking control of the entire kingdom. This marked the establishment of East Breifne as an independent kingdom under their rule, with Mullagh falling within their sphere of influence. They became known as the Princes of East Breifne, a title they held through the Anglo-Norman invasion led by Strongbow in 1172.

The Ó Raghallaighs were renowned castle builders, and their presence in Mullagh is evidenced by their construction of Cloghballybeg Castle on the shores of Mullagh Lake, built by Conor Mór O’Reilly. His son, Conor O’Reilly, also constructed Muff Castle nearby. These fortifications highlight their strategic control over the area, with Mullagh serving as a key location due to its proximity to Mullagh Lake and its position on the Virginia Road. Their chief stronghold was at Tullymongan in Cavan town, but their influence extended across East Breifne, including Mullagh.

The Ó Raghallaighs maintained their territory despite external pressures, eventually surrendering the principality to Queen Elizabeth I in the late 16th century to preserve their lands. Their name, derived from "Raghallach" (meaning "courageous" or "valiant" in Gaelic), reflects their reputation as a formidable lineage. In Mullagh’s early years, they would have been central to local governance, land management, and defence, shaping the region’s medieval history.

Fanny ctd…

William tilled the fields and wove his linen, a trade that kept the pot full when the markets smiled. Mullagh’s fairs, six times a year, were a bustle—traders from Bailieborough and Kells, buying our flax, our eggs … a scrap of cloth. In the year of ’44, a shadow fell upon us, one I’ll never forget, as a cruel disease stole my newly widowed sister, Letitia Kellett Soden, and her two dear wee daughters, Jane and Mary Soden, from this world.

Letitia, was my elder sister, had been widowed and left to raise her children alone in her small corner of County Sligo. Although a strong Kellett woman, losing her husband had worn her thin. Her daughters, Jane and Mary, bright young wee bairns, full of life, so young—

In that spring of 1844—not long after the potato crop began to show signs of trouble, though the true blight was still a year off—a sickness came to Sligo. It was cholera, they said, though some whispered of typhus. The air was heavy with fear, for we’d heard tales of the disease from years before, how it swept through towns like a reaper, sparing none. The doctors called it a miasma, born of foul water and crowded homes, but to us, it felt like a curse. Sligo, with its port and bustling streets, was no stranger to such plagues, and our homes, simple and close-knit, offered little protection.

It began with Letitia. She fell ill sudden-like, gripped by a fever and a terrible sickness in her stomach. She couldn’t keep food down, and her strength faded fast. I went to her, bringing what remedies I knew—boiled water, a bit of broth—but her eyes were sunken, her skin cold. The doctor came too late, muttering about cholera’s merciless speed. Within days, she was gone, her body too weak to fight. I wept for her, my sister, who’d borne so much already.

Jane and Mary, poor lambs, were next. They’d been tending to their mother, fetching water, holding her hand… Perhaps it was the water from the well, tainted by the disease, or the closeness of their care that spread it. Jane fell first, her small frame shaking with chills, her voice calling for her mother even as she slipped away. Mary followed not two days later, her cries quieter, as if she knew her fate. They died within hours of each other, in that same small house, leaving a silence that broke my heart.

The cholera—for that’s what the doctor confirmed it was—struck hard that year. It wasn’t the great outbreak of ’49, but it was fierce in that corner of Sligo. The disease cared not for family or faith, taking both young and old alike. We buried Letitia, Jane, and Mary in the churchyard, a hurried affair, for fear the sickness would spread further. The priest spoke of God’s will, but I could only think of the emptiness left behind.

The deaths of my dear sister Letitia and her daughters left her boys—Edward, Charles, and young Robert—alone in the world. They were just lads. Edward the eldest was only twelve. For poor wee Robert, seven and without his Da, the loss of his mother and sisters nearly broke his tender heart. It was a heavy time in Rathfriland, the land already struggling, with whispers of darker days ahead. Amidst such sorrow, I found a glimmer of joy in giving birth to my own wee daughter, whom I named Letitia to honour my newly departed sister.

Editor Note: Years later, Robert’s marriage to Mary Frances Mortimer would bring a sense of healing, stitching together the bonds that grief had torn apart.

Fanny ctd …

In ’45, the blight crept in, a thief in the night, and the potatoes rotted black, and didn’t our world turn to shadows!

The Famine, ach! It stole more than bread—it snatched hope, neighbours, the laughter of children… The praties, our life’s blood, were muck in the earth. By ’46, hunger clawed at us, fierce as a wolf. I’d wake to the children’s sobs, their faces all bones, and my heart would splinter, with naught but watery porridge to hush them. William, bless him, broke stones on the relief roads for a shilling, but sure, it barely bought the Quaker’s Indian meal, yellow and coarse. I’d stand in the queue, other mammies beside me, Letitia tugging my skirts, too small to know why her belly wailed.

Mullagh’s streets fell silent, like a song forgotten. The fairs faded, the market square bare but for hollow-eyed folk, hands outstretched. The parish, nigh on 6,000 souls, wept for hundreds lost to fever—cholera, typhus, the dysentery that spared none. I saw coffins borne to Garvary Wood, or bodies wrapped in rags when timber ran dry.

The workhouse in Kells, six miles off, was a dread place, its walls a tomb. We vowed we’d never cross its threshold, though the threat hung over us like a storm. As landlords and the Mortimer gentry here, we pressed for rent. Like the Maxwells of Farnham, we had a kinder name, but the middlemen? Och! they’d tumble a cabin for a penny owed.

Being Protestant set us apart. Our ancestral ties to the gentry, through the Mortimers, Ingoldsbys, and Cromwells, shielded us little. Whispers of “soupers”—folk trading faith for food — sowed mistrust. I shared what I had, a fistful of meal and a prayer, with Catholic neighbours, as pain it turns out, knows no church. My children were my stars, and I’d sew by rushlight’s flicker, patching their clothes, teaching our eldest Charles and Mary Frances, and my Soden nephews, their ABCs, dreaming they would all know a world kinder than this.

By ’47, Black ’47, Cavan’s darkest year, Mullagh was a graveyard, its heart stilled. Ships called from Newry, singing of America and Canada, but tales of coffin ships chilled our bones. Friends sailed, their homes left to crumble, and Cavan shrank—243,000 in ’41 to 174,000 by ’51, so they say.

William and I clung on for our babes. I’d wander to the lough, praying for strength, watching herons lift like ghosts over the water. Our Puritan roots, stern as the hills, held us fast—God’s will, we whispered, would carry us through.

As the ’40s waned, the blight softened, but Mullagh wore its wounds. We survived, thinner, wearier, but alive. We dreamed of a new dawn, and in ’63, that dream sailed us to Queensland and the beginning of our Australian Story.

The Story of Our Kellet Ancestors

Our story is rooted in the gentry’s pride, where the Kelletts and Mortimers, steadfast Protestants, held fast to land and faith. In Cavan, amidst the Great Famine’s trials, I spun tales for Mary Frances and Letitia, stories of kin tied to the Cromwells of Huntingdon and the Ingoldsbys of Buckinghamshire. Sir Richard Ingoldsby, born around 1590, stands at the core, his marriage to Elizabeth Cromwell on October 24, 1613, a spark that united two families. Their daughter Anne Ingoldsby carried this legacy to a Mary Chaloner in the 17th century, while Mary “Cherrie” Chaloner, brought the Chaloner legacy into our Kellett fold, enriching our narrative with her courage and grace. This is the story of us — love kindled in unity, tested by war’s shadows, and carried by mothers and daughters to Queensland’s new dawn. It’s a ballad of resilience, lit by women like my mother and great-grandmother, and radiant with Cherrie’s spirit.

The Kelletts and Mortimers, kept our faith strong through tough times. In Cavan, during the Great Famine, I told my children tales of our kin, connected to the famous Cromwells from Huntingdon and the Ingoldsbys from Buckinghamshire. We trace our line back to Sir Oliver Cromwell, our 11th great-grandfather, born around 1562 in Huntingdonshire, England. He was a knight and Member of Parliament, living in the grand Hinchingbrooke House, passed down from his father, Sir Henry Williams, also called Cromwell. Sir Oliver married Elizabeth Bromley, and their daughter, Elizabeth Cromwell, our 10th great-grandmother, married Sir Richard Ingoldsby, linking us to the Ingoldsby family of Lenborough, Buckinghamshire.

Their son, Colonel Sir Richard Ingoldsby, our 9th great-uncle, did something bold—he signed King Charles I’s death warrant in 1649, helping start the Commonwealth of England, a Puritan republic from 1649 to 1653 that ended the monarchy for a time. This brave act tied our family to our cousin Oliver Cromwell, the Lord Protector, who led England during this big change. The Ingoldsbys’ influence spread to Ireland through folks like Sir Henry Ingoldsby, the 1st Baronet, and reached down to our great-grandfather, William, keeping our family’s story alive across the years.

The Tale of Betty and John Chaloner

Now, let me tell you about Betty and John Chaloner, a couple whose love story is a bright part of our family’s tale. Betty, called Lady Betty Parsons, was born in 1675 in Birr, County Offaly, Ireland, to Katherine Lacy and Sir William Parsons, a baronet who owned the grand Birr Castle. John Chaloner, born in 1658 in Guisborough, Yorkshire, England, was a preacher, a Rev. Chaplain who served on the big Royal Navy ship HMS Royal Sovereign and worked in Garvagh, County Londonderry, and later settled in Rathmore, Moynalty, County Meath, Ireland.

Their love began in 1695 at a fancy ball in Dublin, where important families from Ireland and England met. I can picture Betty, glowing in her dress, catching John’s eye as he stood proud, his preacher’s heart warmed by her smile. They fell in love and married, joining Ireland’s gentry with England’s, like a bridge between two worlds. Betty was kind, always helping the poor in Birr with food and blankets. John was brave, praying with sailors on the Royal Sovereign during stormy seas and preaching in Garvagh to help people heal after hard times. Later, they moved to Rathmore, Meath, where John kept preaching, and Betty cared for their home.

They had six children—four boys and two girls, including their daughter Mary, nicknamed “Cherrie,” born in 1700 when Betty was 25 and John was 42. Cherrie was special to our family because she married a Kellett man around 1720, bringing her parents’ story into our Kellett line. Life wasn’t always easy for Betty and John. In 1703, their son William got very sick with a fever, and they worried he might not make it. But Betty nursed him, and John prayed, and William got better, making their family stronger.

The Story of Great Grandmother Elizabeth Cromwell

I am Elizabeth Cromwell, born around 1595 in the golden halls of Hinchingbrooke House, Huntingdon. My life has been a tapestry of love’s warmth, war’s shadow, and a legacy that stretches beyond my years. As the daughter of Sir Oliver Cromwell and Elizabeth Bromley, sister to many, and wife to Sir Richard Ingoldsby, I’ve seen our family’s story unfold through feasts and follies, triumphs and trials. This is the story of us—my parents, my father’s second wife Anne, my uncle Robert, my cousin Oliver, and my Ingoldsby kin—a tale of sparks that lit our path and twists that tested our hearts.

My Father, Sir Oliver, and the Golden Legacy

My father, Sir Oliver Cromwell, was a man of boundless dreams, born around 1562 to Sir Henry Cromwell, the “Golden Knight.” Grandfather Henry’s wealth, built on lands from the Dissolution, made Hinchingbrooke a palace of splendour. Father inherited his father’s flair, hosting kings and lords with feasts that echoed through our halls. I remember him in his prime, knighted by Queen Elizabeth in 1598, a High Sheriff and MP, his voice carrying our shire’s hopes. But his heart was too grand for his purse, and debts shadowed our home.

Father’s love for grandeur came from Grandfather Henry, who died in 1604, leaving us a legacy of honour and a warning: gold glitters, but it slips away. I was a child then, watching Father strive to match that shine, his eyes alight with ambition. Yet I saw the strain, the cost of keeping Hinchingbrooke’s fires burning, and I learned early that love and legacy come with a price.

My Mother, Elizabeth Bromley, the Heart of Our Home

My mother, Elizabeth Bromley, was the steady flame in our family’s hearth. Born around 1566, daughter of Lord Chancellor Sir Thomas Bromley, she married Father before 1586, bringing wisdom and grace to Hinchingbrooke. She bore thirteen of us—Henry, Thomas, John, myself, and others—each birth a testament to her strength. I see her still, managing our bustling household, her quiet resolve balancing Father’s extravagance. She taught me to read, to pray, and to hold fast to family, no matter the storm.

Mother’s hands wove our lives together, but in 1603, when I was just a girl, she faded. Perhaps the toll of so many children, or some silent sickness, stole her from us. Her death left Hinchingbrooke hollow, and Father’s eyes dimmed. I was too young to grasp the void, but I carried her lessons forward, vowing to be a strength for my own family one day. Her loss was our first great twist, a chapter closed too soon.

Father’s Second Chapter with Anne Hooftman

After Mother’s passing, Father found love again with Anne Hooftman, a widow of wealth and spirit. Anne, once married to the financier Sir Horatio Palavicini, brought her three sons and a fortune to our home. She was different from Mother—worldly, vibrant, her Antwerp roots added new colours to our story. She bore Father four children—Oliver, Mary, Giles, and Anna—blending our families with care. I admired her, a stepmother who treated us as her own, her laughter filling the halls where Mother’s voice had faded.

Anne’s wealth eased Father’s debts, but his feasts continued, hosting King James in 1617. I helped her manage the household, learning to balance love and duty. Alas, in 1620, Anne, too, was taken, her health failed. Father stood alone again, and I, now a young woman, felt the weight of our family’s fragility. Anne’s spark had burned bright, but her loss reminded me that even the strongest flames can flicker out.

Uncle Robert and Cousin Oliver: A Family Divided

My uncle Robert Cromwell, Father’s younger brother, lived a quieter life in Huntingdon, married to Elizabeth Steward. Their son, my cousin Oliver, born in 1599, was my father’s godson, a boy with a fire I didn’t yet understand. Uncle Robert, a justice of the peace, died in 1617, leaving his family to forge their own path. Oliver, though, was destined for more. By the 1640s, as England tore itself apart in civil war, he rose as a Parliamentarian leader, his name shaking the kingdom.

Father, a Royalist at heart, clashed with Oliver’s cause. I remember the pain of 1643, when Ramsey Abbey, our family’s retreat, was searched for arms, and 1644, when Father was briefly imprisoned. Oliver’s rise to Lord Protector in 1653 was a marvel, but it divided us. I loved my cousin, but his path broke our family’s heart, a twist that mirrored England’s wounds. Yet his strength, born of our shared blood, made me proud, even as I mourned our fractured bond.

My Life with Sir Richard Ingoldsby: A New Legacy

In 1613, I married Sir Richard Ingoldsby, a Buckinghamshire knight whose family hailed from Lincolnshire’s gentry. Our wedding in Huntingdon was a spark of joy, tying the Cromwells to the Ingoldsbys. Richard, born around 1590, was kind and ambitious, and together we built a life, raising fourteen children, including our sons Richard and Henry, who would carry our name through war and change.

Our son Colonel Sir Richard Ingoldsby, born in 1617, became a Parliamentarian, serving under cousin Oliver and signing King Charles I’s death warrant as a regicide. His courage, and his survival of the Restoration with a knighthood in 1662, filled me with awe. Our son Sir Henry, a baronet, steadied our family’s place in Buckinghamshire and Ireland, his lands a new chapter. Our grandson John Ingoldsby sailed to Boston, planting our legacy across the sea.

The Commonwealth: Our Family’s Stage

The Commonwealth, from 1649 to 1660, was the crucible of our story. After King Charles I’s execution, England became a republic, with cousin Oliver at its heart. As Lord Protector from 1653, he ruled with an iron hand, his conquests in Ireland and Scotland shaping a turbulent era. My son Richard’s role as a regicide tied us to this upheaval, while Father’s Royalist leanings and imprisonment showed our family’s divisions. The Commonwealth’s end in 1660, with Charles II’s restoration, closed a chapter, but our legacy lived on through my children and Oliver’s name.

The Final Page

By 1655, Father died at Ramsey Abbey, aged 93, his dreams in later years were of were of Hinchingbrooke, sold to pay debts. Cousin Oliver, the Lord Protector, shone brightest, but I like to think my Ingoldsby children carried our spark further. From Father’s golden feasts to Mother’s quiet strength, from Anne’s vitality to Oliver’s iron will, our story is one of us—flawed, fierce, and bound by love.

The Story of Us: A Cromwell and Ingoldsby Tale

The Tale as told by Great Grandfather Sir Oliver Cromwell

I’m Sir Oliver Cromwell, standing in the halls of Hinchingbrooke House, where the walls hum with the echoes of my life’s highs and lows. Born around 1562 in Huntingdon, I’ve lived a story that feels like a ballad—full of love’s fire, heartbreak’s sting, and a legacy that twists through England’s heart. This is the story of us: my father Sir Henry, my wives Elizabeth Bromley and Anne Hooftman, my brother Robert, my nephew Oliver, who’d one day rule this land, and my daughter Elizabeth, whose marriage to Sir Richard Ingoldsby tied our name to another. It’s a tale of feasts and debts, of loyalty and division, and of a spark that never quite fades.

Chapter One: The Golden Days with My Father

My father, Sir Henry Cromwell, was the sun of our world, the “Golden Knight” whose wealth lit up Huntingdonshire. Born around 1537, he inherited lands from my grandfather Sir Richard, who’d won favour under King Henry VIII. Hinchingbrooke, once a nunnery, became our castle, its towers reaching for glory. Father married Joan Warren, a gentle soul, and they raised eight of us—me, Robert, and our siblings. He threw feasts that drew lords from across the realm, his laughter filling the air. I was his heir, the boy who dreamed of matching his shine.

But gold slips through fingers. Father’s generosity outpaced his purse, and when he died in 1604, he left me Hinchingbrooke, a seat in Parliament, and a lesson: splendour comes at a cost. I was 42, a knight since ’98, and ready to write my chapter. The spark was there, but I didn’t see the twist coming.

Chapter Two: Elizabeth Bromley, My First Love

I met Elizabeth Bromley in my youth, her eyes sharp with wit, her heart steady as the stars. Daughter of Sir Thomas Bromley, Lord Chancellor, she was my match in ambition. We wed before 1586, and she filled Hinchingbrooke with life. Our love was a fire, burning bright as we raised thirteen children—Henry, Thomas, John, and our Elizabeth, who’d one day carry our name to the Ingoldsbys. Each birth tested her, but she never faltered, managing our home while I chased dreams of grandeur.

I hosted King James I in 1603, my feasts rivalling my father’s. Elizabeth warned me of the cost, but I couldn’t stop. I was High Sheriff, a voice in Parliament, knighted by Queen Elizabeth herself. Yet, that year, the spark flickered… Elizabeth fell ill, her strength sapped, perhaps by the weight of our family. She died, leaving me alone in a house too quiet, my heart a book slammed shut. I didn’t know how to turn the page.

Chapter Three: Anne Hooftman, A New Verse

Then, off to finishing school in Switzerland, where I picked up French, learned to skate and ski—useless back in Ireland, mind you, unless the lake froze over, which was a rare treat.

Back home, June and I threw ourselves into horses. We’d buy young ones, train them up, hunt, show jump, and sell them on. One year, I took my darling Zircon, hitched to his trap, to the local station, loaded him onto the train to Dublin, and drove him across the city to Ballsbridge for the Dublin Showground. All week, we jumped, and I stayed with friends, dancing at the Show parties every night. Then, we trundled back home the same way—what a lark!

In 1945, June’s friend Nancy brought her brother Peter, a dashing Naval Officer, to Lakeview. Well, he took one look at me and was smitten, but my parents weren’t so sure a naval life would suit me. They made us wait two whole years before we could marry in 1947. Oh, what a life we had after that—nine homes we moved to, following Peter’s career! I learned to live in towns, even abroad, often alone with our four darlings—Katharine, Gerald, Philippa, and Anthony—while Peter was off at sea for months. But I made our home a warm, happy place, always room for extra children, cousins, or friends on holiday. Every summer, I’d take the lot back to Lakeview, letting them taste the freedom I’d known. I never worked a job, you see—my joy was in my family, our dogs, ponies, my garden, and cooking. Feeding a crowd of hungry children? Easy as pie. Now, those grand naval dinner parties in Caithness and Washington DC in the ‘50s and ‘60s? Those were a bit daunting, I’ll admit.

When Peter retired in 1975, we chose Grasspark in North Devon—my first time picking a home! Those 40 hilly acres were a delight, with our animals and livestock. I got back to riding, and oh, the joy of having my children and grandchildren visit. But life wasn’t all sunshine. Losing my boys, Gerald in 1988 and Anthony in 2002, broke my heart. I wrote in my diary, 'I cried every day for more than a year, such very dear boys. The death of one’s child is very deep, but one pays a high price for love.' I couldn’t fathom why God would allow it, not once but twice.

In my later years, arthritis got the better of me. I couldn’t get about—too painful to climb steps or even get in a car. But I didn’t like to fuss. If you asked how I was, I’d just ask about the day outside instead. Family and friends kept me going, especially my dear Olivia, my great-niece Fiona, and my 11 grandchildren and six great-grandchildren. My eyes weren’t what they used to be, but I loved seeing my garden’s flowers and hearing the birds. I knitted, made tapestry cushions for everyone, wrote letters, and read big-print books. When Peter passed in 2009, after 62 years together, I was sad but glad his pain was done. Those last seven years, I had over 40 carers come and go. I kept little notes on them, scoring them out of 10—made it easy to know who I’d have back!

Looking back, I hope I left a bit of love and kindness in this world. I tried to see the good in everyone, to laugh and keep that twinkle in my eye, and to put others first. My family carries me in their hearts, and if my life inspires even a little, well, that’s enough for me."

The Mary Francis Soden (nee Mortimer) Story

I’m Mary Frances Soden, born Mary Mortimer, on the 29th December 1841 in Mullagh, County Cavan, Ireland. Daughter of William and Fanny. Granddaughter of David.

My heart still aches for those green hills, where I grew up in a Protestant family, tied to the Church of Ireland. My father, William, and mother, Fanny Kellett, raised us on tales of our Mortimer kin—Norman blood, Viking roots, and land granted by Cromwell in the 1650s. My grandfather, David, farmed 75 acres at Cloughballymore, a gentleman in his own right but by 1862, at 22, I had been wed to Robert Dawson Soden in Balbriggan, County Dublin and it was plain to us all that Ireland was no longer the land of promise it once seemed … The Great Famine’s shadow lingered, though it had ended years before. The linen trade, Cavan’s lifeblood, was crumbling, and even families like ours, with land, felt the pinch. There was little for a young couple to build on. Robert, orphaned as a seven year old would always grieve for his family despite he and his 2 brothers having Kellet kin to turn to.

Word came of Australia—Queensland, they said, needed hands to work the land, offering passage for just £5 a head. My parents, Fanny and William and our brothers and me sisters set their hearts on it. We all saw a chance for a better life, far from the loss, hunger and want.

So, we sold what we could—linens, furniture, bits of family keepsakes—and left Mullagh’s lake behind. I’ll never forget the ache of that farewell, knowing I’d likely never see Cavan again.

We made our way to Liverpool, where the Cairngorm, a Black Ball ship, waited. On March 22, 1863, we boarded, 153 souls from Liverpool, joined by 187 more at Greenock, 340 in all. The ship sailed on March 28, bound for Brisbane, a 92-day journey. Robert and I were packed into steerage—cramped, damp quarters that smelled of salt and fear. The ship was a “wet” one, they said, and the damp took its toll. Measles struck early, claiming seven lives, mostly children. A case of whooping cough flared, and five others died—consumption, water on the brain. Twelve gone by journey’s end. Yet, the seas were kind for most of the way, save for rough waters near Australia’s coast.

I carried a secret burden—our first child, growing inside me. On April 6, just days out from Greenock, the pains came. I was terrified, labouring in that swaying ship, but the midwife and my mother, Fanny, were my strength. When Kate was born, one of three babes born on the voyage, I held her close, her tiny warmth a spark of joy amidst the grief around us. I swore we’d make it to Australia, for her, for us. Life on the Cairngorm settled into a rhythm—hard tack and salt pork for meals, stolen walks on deck, and evenings brightened by a Cavan fiddler’s tunes. Flying fish and whales broke the monotony, glimpses of God’s wonders that kept my spirits up.

On July 2, 1863, we sighted Brisbane Roads, Moreton Bay. Dr. Hobbs, the Health Officer, cleared us, and we stepped onto Queensland’s soil, weary but alive. We stayed at the immigrant depot before settling at Pine Mountain, later moving to Mt Beppo. There, Robert and I farmed and raised our family—eleven children in all, five boys and five girls. Kate the first, the rest were born between 1863 and 1886. My parents settled nearby, and my sister Letitia was wed to Alexander Martin in Brisbane in 1866, putting down roots near Bundaberg. Queensland was nothing like Cavan’s rolling hills—but it held hope, a chance to build something new.

I’ve never lost the lilt of Cavan in my voice, nor the stories of our Mortimer line—knights, Vikings, Cromwell’s men. Ireland lives in me, but Australia is home. The Cairngorm carried us across oceans, through sickness and storms, to a future we dared to dream. Robert and I built a legacy here, our children growing strong on this new land. That journey, hard as it was, made us who we are—resilient, hopeful, and forever tied to two worlds.

The Story of Us: A Tapestry of Legacy

by the Great Grandfathers

As William Mortimer, born in 1805 in the green hills of County Cavan, Ireland, a man whose life was shaped by the land and the faith of my forebears, standing beside my beloved Fanny Kellett, born in 1810, whom I married in 1837 at St. Michan’s in Dublin, I now look forward to the tales to be told in the Australian Story.

Together, Fanny and I raised five children in Mullagh, enduring the trials of the Great Famine before seeking a new life in Queensland, Australia, in 1863. As I reflect on our place in this vast ancestry tapestry, I see the Mortimers as a thread of resilience, woven into a greater fabric that stretches back to the English gentry of the Cromwells and Ingoldsbys, and binds us to the steadfast Kelletts. Our journey, from the fields of Cavan to the shores of Moreton Bay, is but one strand in The Story of Us, a tale of faith, fortitude, and family that spans centuries and continents. Let me guide you, dear kin, as we wrap up these storylines, tracing the legacy that unites us all.

The Cromwells: A Legacy of Ambition and Faith

The Cromwell storyline begins with Sir Oliver Cromwell, born in 1562 in Huntingdon, England, a man whose ambition and faith set the foundation for our family’s journey. Sir Oliver, knighted in 1598, inherited Hinchingbrooke House from his father, Sir Henry Cromwell, the “Golden Knight,” whose wealth from the Dissolution of the Monasteries built a legacy of grandeur. Sir Oliver married Elizabeth Bromley, and their daughter, Elizabeth Cromwell, born around 1595, became the bridge to our lineage through her marriage to Sir Richard Ingoldsby. Sir Oliver’s nephew, Oliver Cromwell, the Lord Protector (1599–1658), reshaped England and Ireland with his Commonwealth and the Cromwellian conquest, granting lands to Protestant families, a legacy that influenced our own path in Cavan.

The Cromwells’ ambition and Puritan zeal flowed through their descendants, shaping a family that stood firm through England’s civil wars and beyond. Their legacy of leadership and faith, rooted in Hinchingbrooke’s halls, became a beacon for generations, a thread of determination that wove its way into our story, inspiring us to seek new horizons when the famine struck.

The Ingoldsbys: A Bridge Across Lands – Sir Richard

The Ingoldsby storyline is mine to tell … it intertwines with the Cromwells through my wife Elizabeth, whose marriage to me before 1614 bound our fates. Our sons—Colonel Sir Richard Ingoldsby (1617–1685), Sir Henry Ingoldsby, 1st Baronet (1622–1701), and John Ingoldsby (1621–c. 1684)—carried our name across England, Ireland, and America, their lives a testament to resilience and adaptability.

Colonel Sir Richard, a man of fierce conviction, embodied the Puritan resolve of his Cromwell kin, joining the Parliamentarian cause in the English Civil War. As a Colonel in the New Model Army, he fought at Bridgwater and Bristol, but his most fateful act came in 1649 when he signed King Charles I’s death warrant, one of 59 regicides to do so. His Parliamentarian leanings, driven by opposition to the king’s policies, marked him as a radical, though he later claimed Oliver Cromwell coerced him into signing—a tale I heard with scepticism. At the Restoration in 1660, Richard’s pragmatic shift to Royalism, supporting General Monck and capturing John Lambert, secured his pardon. Charles II knighted him a Knight of the Bath in 1661, and he served as MP for Aylesbury until he died in 1685, buried in Hartwell Church, Buckinghamshire. His descendants in England continued our legacy, a thread of political service and redemption.

Sir Henry took our name to Ireland, initially serving Charles I before pledging loyalty to Oliver Cromwell. He fought in the 1649 Irish campaign at Drogheda and Limerick, becoming Governor of Limerick, and earned lands in County Clare and Meath. His political career saw him as MP for Clare, Limerick, and Kerry, and later in the Irish House of Commons, his adaptability securing his baronetcy of Beggstown, County Meath, at the Restoration. Henry raised a regiment for William III in 1689, serving until his death in 1701, leaving an Irish line that flourished through his son George. His descendants in Ireland became a cornerstone of our family’s presence in the Protestant gentry.

John ventured to Boston, Massachusetts, by 1640, marrying Ruth Griffin before 1649 and founding the American Ingoldsby line. His descendants spread across the New World, a testament to the courage it took to build a new life far from England’s conflicts. Together, our sons’ descendants became a bridge to distant lands, their paths weaving our legacy across continents.

The Kelletts: Resilience in Cavan and Beyond – Sir Oliver

The Kellett storyline brings us closer to my heart through my beloved Fanny, born in 1810 in Lurgan, County Cavan. Fanny’s family, rooted in Cavan’s Protestant community, descends from John Chaloner, a reverend born in 1652, who married Betty Parsons in 1700. John’s mother, Anne Ingoldsby, was the daughter of Elizabeth Cromwell and myself, linking the Kelletts to our Cromwell-Ingoldsby heritage. John and Betty’s daughter, Cherrie Kellett, carried the name forward, and through her, our lineage flows to Mary Chaloner, Fanny’s 6th great-grandmother, who bore Jane Kellett, mother of Margaret Young.

Continued by William ..

Fanny and I raised five children in Mullagh, including Mary Frances, born in 1841, and Letitia, born in 1844. Our life in Cavan was shaped by the agrarian economy, reliant on potatoes and linen, until the Great Famine (1845–1852) brought devastation. The famine’s toll, with Cavan’s population dropping nearly 30%, pushed us to seek a new life. In 1863, we boarded the Cairngorm under Queensland’s assisted immigration scheme, arriving in Moreton Bay on September 29 after a three-month voyage. Mary Frances, married to Robert Dawson Soden, gave birth to our granddaughter Kate at sea, a symbol of hope. Letitia, who married Alexander Martin Sr. in 1866, settled in Barolin, near Bundaberg, contributing to the sugar industry. Our other children, including Charles Mortimer, born around 1839, also sought new paths, with Charles arriving in Queensland on the Queen of the Colonies in 1865.

The Mortimers: A Thread of Resilience by Mary Frances

The Mortimers’ storyline, my own, is one of endurance and renewal. In Mullagh, we faced the famine’s hardships, our Protestant faith—tied to the Ingoldsbys’ Puritan resolve through Elizabeth Cromwell’s 1613 marriage — giving us strength. The promise of land in Queensland drove our emigration, firstly with Robert, baby Catherine (Kate) and me, Ma and Da, and soon followed by Letitia, we established our Australian lineage in Pine Mountain in the Brisbane Valley and Barolin near Bundaberg. Charles, at 26, sought his own path, arriving in Queensland to contribute to the colony’s growth as to our other siblings and kin over time.

Uniting the Tapestry: A Legacy Across Time – Sir Richard

The Story of Us weaves these threads into a unified legacy. The Cromwells’ ambition and faith, rooted in Sir Oliver’s grandeur, set the stage for our journey, their Puritan ideals echoing through the generations. The Ingoldsbys, through my sons Richard, Henry, and John, bridged England, Ireland, and America, their resilience and adaptability shaping our family’s path. The Kelletts, through Fanny’s steadfast spirit, carried this heritage to Cavan, their faith sustaining us through famine and emigration. The Mortimers, as the final thread, brought these legacies to Australia, where our descendants—like Robin Truasheim and Peter McKean, united in marriage in 1975 at St Andrew’s Anglican Church in South Brisbane—discovered in 2025 their shared descent from Fanny and me, a revelation that binds our past to your present.

Our tapestry, spanning centuries and continents, is one of faith, fortitude, and family. From Hinchingbrooke’s halls to Cavan’s fields, from Limerick’s governance to Boston’s frontier, and finally to Queensland’s shores, The Story of Us endures—a legacy of love and perseverance that lives on in you, our cherished descendants.
